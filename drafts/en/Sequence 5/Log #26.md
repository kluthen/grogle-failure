***Gus***
> Good morning, master. Six hours on the dot, as agreed. {{ sun — in theory }}

No sun, obviously. Just the wan neon of the stockroom and the same smell of cold oil. But I slept — really slept, straight through — and my body thanks me for it: not a single ache, the nanomachine gauge back up, my thoughts almost clear. Around me, the basement shakes itself awake. Santu kept the drone-truck warm all night. We load the wounded, wedge in the bundles, and the convoy lurches off into the gloom of the drone-roads. Somewhere above our heads, it's the morning of the second day.

The truck-drone hobbles. That's really the right word. It hobbles — cruising speed of a hurried pedestrian, chassis rebuilt from bits and scraps, one of the rear motors squealing at irregular intervals like an animal someone forgot to finish off. In the back, the survivors and their bundles sway with every bump. It smells of hot rubber, drone oil, and warm metal — not very romantic, but after the night we just spent in a basement that pooled the sweat and fear of forty-odd crammed-in people, you adjust your standards.

Other drones overtake us regularly. Most circulate politely. Three or four required a bit of negotiation not to end up embedded in our flank. Crist fried the sensors of two of them with the air of someone discovering a new toy — sly smile, calibrated discharge, he looks genuinely pleased with himself every time. I blocked the third bare-handed while Santu flipped the route priority from the interface.

At the back of the truck, Yannick gripped the makeshift railing, eyes riveted on Crist with unreserved admiration. Seven questions since we left. Two answers obtained. Absolute record. For the record, this kid used to look at me with the wariness reserved for vacuum cleaners and overly tall strangers. Now he's hanging off the heels of the guy who throws electric arcs with his fingers. Kid logic. I'm almost jealous. Almost.

Lou, for her part, hasn't granted Crist the slightest glance. She gave me her briefing twenty minutes ago with the seriousness of an intelligence officer.

"If we can "level up our attributes," we shouldn't drag our feet."

She had the tone of someone quoting an authority. The authority's name is Jaz. I nodded. For my part I genuinely appreciate my new musculature — borderline weightlifter without ever having set foot in a gym. I'd just have liked to go through checkout without the "homicidal urge and bloodbath included" option. I keep that to myself.

Santu put the trip to good use. With the scrap metal salvaged from the crashed drone, he assembled me what he calls an improvised tetsubo — two steel tubes welded together, reinforced with rods and nuts, a grip wrapped in rags. Heavy, uncertain hold, and I sense the thing's going to slip out of my fingers if I strike too hard. But it looks solid. Jaz approved with his virtual can, with the moderate enthusiasm of a trainer making do with what he's got. I trust him by default.

The repair center appears at the end of the road, and the first thing that catches the eye is the light. In these galleries juggling between the yellowish cones of the fixtures and pure black, the place shines like a shop window — doors wide open, neon at full power, the yellow-and-black mechanical arms gleaming at the ceiling. Inside: shelves floor to ceiling loaded with spare parts, fluid canisters, coiled cables, a hydraulic lift. And at the back, clearly visible from the road, the elevator on its raised platform right next to the controller's glass booth. Our ticket to the surface.

What's less appealing is the dozen drones piled in front of the entrance. Most look in bad shape — dented, caved in, doors ripped off. A few impacts happened right here, judging by the traces of fresh paint on the concrete. And speaking of paint. A three-meter space in front of the storefront is immaculate — spotless floor, clean edges, yellow and black freshly redone. The center doesn't want new customers. It decided that the immaculate was a message clear enough.

Santu deactivates our motor. The truck slows, sways once, stops right in front of the heap of debris.

Nobody gets out. The tension is high, all eyes riveted on this strange tableau.

"What's the plan?" I let slip, jumping out first anyway. Because watching the mechanical arms from the back of a truck doesn't solve much. It's clearly a trap.

Fara squeezes the trigger. First shot: deflects a crate of parts at the back — dull sound of tipped metal. Second shot: bullseye. Part of the drone explodes, smoke rises, and the rest catches fire all at once. The articulated stalk holding it starts wriggling as if seized by existential panic.

The other arms and drones stir. The fire-suppression system kicks in — what's left of the little drone is hurled away in a spray of flame-retardant foam.
