# Log A

"Madam Deputy," Mister Mertens throws at me, "are we nearly safe?"
His emotional voice and his Dutch accent annoy me. Until now, I'd mostly been focused on the creak of the freight lift carrying us down to the basements. I was in my bubble. *Are we safe?* I don't know. In a few dozen meters, I'll be able to answer that question. For now, I'd rather keep quiet. My heart's pounding. I still have a hard time controlling my breathing. It's as if I'd run a marathon. I'm lost. I think back to the horror we just lived through and that others are still living. I barely had time to realize they were coming after me. Fear provokes such different reactions. Me, my legs carried me — I was lucky — but I saw people incapable of the slightest defensive move, ready to welcome death with open arms.
"Madam Deputy?" Mister Mertens insists.
Even during the protests against immigration, thirty years ago, I hadn't seen violence like this. I flush from my mind the image of a man who chased me through the Town Hall corridors, then I let out a good breath. I lie.
"Sorry, sorry… We're already safe!"
Under the trembling neon, nine pairs of eyes finally relax. Their shoulders drop and their faces sag. Without their AR projections — their flamboyant clothes, their virtual hairstyles, and the intangible objects floating around them — they look resigned. As for Mister Mertens, an old man from the technical service, he nods with a smile, then sits down in a corner. With those wrinkles and his work overalls, he looks like he belongs to another time. I watch him close his eyes. He seems happy to have gotten his answer. My head's spinning. I hold onto the freight lift doors. Mister Mertens is the only person familiar to me here. All the others are survivors I managed to gather. The Mayor isn't among them. She *fell* before my eyes while we were in the middle of a meeting. I think her blood stains my clothes. With the tip of my thumb, I try to clean the fabric, but I stop. My gaze loses itself in the void. I'm no longer here. I'm back there, behind us, a few hours ago. I have no idea how I came out of all that alive, but I came out of it. I'm back there, behind us, then I come to my senses. I'm in front of them, in front of all those I'm responsible for. I have to hold on. They trust me. They're my fellow citizens, I'm the only representative of the State and the only one here who knows the bunker access protocols.
I turn around to observe them in silence.
"Do you think it's the end of the world?" a disheveled woman throws out.
A man takes her in his arms. He repeats my lie. He believes it.
"You heard: we're safe!"
The woman's eyes light up.
"My daughter's waiting for me at the daycare."
"Let's hope she doesn't wait too long," the man adds.
Then he holds her a little tighter. I feel guilty. I'm even a bit ashamed not to stop them in their delusion, but I have no other solution to keep everyone calm. I need time to make sure I have control of the situation. Every year, we run simulations within the administration. We're assisted by an AI that guides us according to this or that event. A few minutes ago, I tried to connect to that AI, to the bunker control room, but it didn't appear, it didn't answer. It terrifies me: not only do I have to hold the reins of the city when I was never trained for it, but on top of that I'm facing temperamental logistical resources. I feel like I'm crossing a nightmare. I'm suffocating. I hide from the others. This cab may have been designed to bear the weight of two hundred people, but it already makes me claustrophobic. I can't even imagine what it'll be like several meters underground. I feel the tears rising. I hold them back. My mouth is dry. I mechanically fix my bun to keep up appearances. I'm not a child anymore. I shouldn't be trembling like this. I mop my forehead with my sleeve, then I fix my gaze on the floor number. A scream pulls me from my preoccupations. A woman has isolated herself. Her eyes are searching for something. She's surely focused on some interface, but I wonder if she isn't simply reliving the night's horrors. People avoid meeting her gaze as she wraps herself in her own arms and rocks. I get the feeling she's lost her mind. I step up to help her, but a man with graying temples who, until now, had been pacing back and forth, steps between us.
He grabs my wrist.
"I hope you're going to sort this out quickly," he throws at me.
"Sort out what?"
I struggle: he lets go and gets angrier still.
"Where were you these past few hours? I've got a company to run…"
No one steps in, but the curious ones prick up their ears.
"I don't know what to tell you," I admit to him.
"That's the last straw!"
He seems perfectly aware that the situation is out of the ordinary, but his priorities are very different from ours. I take a step back. He's a head taller than me. I get the feeling he could turn violent. And as his words swell with insults toward the government, I wonder why he holds, as if his life depended on it, a Vuitton paper bag full of clothes. An AR window interrupts us. The freight lift stops. We've reached the Command Center, but to my great surprise the doors stay shut.
"What are you waiting for to open up for us? my interlocutor panics."
A second one speaks up before I can even answer.
"We're not going to stay stuck this close to the goal, are we?"
"Give me three seconds and stay calm, please!"
Suddenly, the virtual projection of a face cuts everyone's urge to complain short. The frightened people press to the back of the cab. I'm left alone facing this gigantic head that covers its whole width. Its bowl cut, without a single stray lock, frames androgynous features and almond eyes. Those curves are perfect and the vertical line tattooed in the middle of its forehead is recognizable among a thousand. It's Siska, the AI I'd desperately tried to contact, five minutes earlier. When it comes to life, I feel the people shudder.
"Hello," it throws at us.
The movement of its lips is jerky, but the speakers spit out a voice perfectly imitating the human voice. The survivors press a little closer together. Mister Mertens doesn't move. He stays seated near the doors. I suddenly feel Siska's immense eyes fixing on me as if I were facing the judgment of an ancient god. I feel threatened, even though I know it's intangible.
Its mouth activates again.
"Madam Payet, this floor is reserved for level 4 personnel."
I tense up, but I don't lose my composure.
"I acquired these rights following the death of the Mayor."
"The Mayor is still alive," Siska retorts.
Its eyes close, the line on its forehead blinks, and an AR window opens on the right-hand wall. It's a direct feed from the city council's surveillance cameras. Amid the overturned chairs, among the corpses of the deputies and our most loyal citizens, impossible creatures wander and feed on human flesh. The survivors accompanying me hide their faces, but I'm forced to note that Siska is right. An AR label floats above one of these monstrosities. A rush of heat explodes in my skull. In a certain sense, the Mayor is alive, but I have the terrible feeling of sinking a little deeper into the nightmare. There's still a solution, though.
"Siska, I'd like to initiate a removal-from-office procedure."
"Very well, Madam Payet. Please wait!"
The immense face before us opens its eyes again and, by the same token, cuts the camera feed. An org chart draws itself in place of the videos. It represents the whole council. A photo of the Mayor sits enthroned at the top. Those of my colleagues, below, are mostly tinted gray with a black band crossing the corner. The autonomous system has already taken their deaths into account. Under other photos, a symbol indicates that communication is impossible. In the end, I really am the only representative of the State who can save the situation; Siska shouldn't hold out long.
One of the survivors steps closer. His grimace bears witness to his apprehension.
"What's all this removal-from-office business? How can it come to this?"
"I think we're facing a procedural gap," I explain.
His mouth stays open in astonishment.
"Are you telling us we showed up here without permission?"
"This is a joke, I hope!" a second reacts.
The woman worried about the end of the world joins in too.
"Is what's happening to us really that serious, then?"
Her complexion goes pale at my silence. The angry one comes back over.
"Of course it's serious, you stupid cow!"
The woman shrinks. I'm afraid he'll end up hitting her, but he holds back.
"You ever seen humans eat each other when everything was fine?"
Another survivor lets out a nervous laugh.
"So much, damn it!" he throws. We're in deep shit here...
I take a deep breath and step forward.
"Please!"
My voice echoes off the cab walls.
"I'd like everyone to stay calm."
The faces twist. They want to speak. I fire back.
"I think you've all noticed we're facing an out-of-the-ordinary event and, even though I completely understand your distress, we mustn't turn against one another. There are more urgent matters!"
I don't give them time to contradict me and I press on.
"For now, I know as much as you do about the severity of the problem that brought us here, and everything I can tell you comes from my own conclusions. You've surely got the same ones, by the way: a terrorist attack, a global malfunction in our vital-sign monitoring systems. It's also possible this catastrophe comes from elsewhere. I'm no expert. Anything is conceivable…"
I think my dithering terrifies them. I close my eyes.
"Listen!" I add. Just like you, I'm still in shock.
At these words, my hands tremble; I hide them behind my back.
"And yet, I'll say it again: we're safe. You have no news of your loved ones, you've felt trapped since the communication network went down. I understand that very well! Just know that the bunker is here to remedy all that. Siska has the capacity to learn and manage new situations by analyzing an impressive amount of data. It's going to surprise you, I'm sure. In under five minutes, it'll grant me the necessary authorizations and we'll open a secure channel to the outside of the city. We'll then have an overview of the situation. Does that work for you?"
Everyone nods, except the angry one who challenges me with his gaze.
"I'll give you five minutes, then!" he throws at me.
That's all it takes for a message to display in front of us.
"Ha!" I exclaim, victorious.
The man frowns.
"I just received an answer!"

Siska freezes. The band on its forehead blinks, then the org chart displayed on the wall updates. From one of its branches, a new line traces itself to the photo of a scrawny stranger with long hair and a bushy beard. His face rings a bell. I've crossed paths with him several times near the coffee printer, in the Town Hall corridors, when I'd finish late at night. His name is Jochem Maas. He's a run-of-the-mill employee, at the very bottom of the ladder, surely the son of a Dutch immigrant straight out of the slums who benefited from one of the positions we reserve for minorities. It disgusts me. I can't let this slide and, as I'm about to challenge this absurdity, Siska cuts the ground from under me. It retorts instantly, out loud, as if to remind me that the only competent authority here is it and no one else.
"There is no alternative, Madam Payet."
I protest.
"This man isn't even among us!"
"As long as he's connected to the bunker network, he's part of the process."
The survivors start stirring at my back.
"It's therefore time to vote," Siska announces.
Its forehead blinks again and, with it, the cab walls light up with a milky veil that blinds me. When I finally manage to open my eyelids, AR furniture has risen before our eyes. I'm disoriented. The room has turned into a replica of a polling station with its booths and its ballot box. My hips are caught in the projection of a table covered with ballots and envelopes. I move and all the survivors standing in the wrong place move with me. We gather in the center. Siska's gigantic head has vanished, but the AI is still here. It projects its presence into a virtual body with feminine, voluptuous forms emitting a bluish aura. To my great surprise, it wears a full lycra outfit, the kind of skintight outfit you couldn't wear without looking vulgar. I don't know what's gotten into it. Siska has no reason to behave like this or to individualize itself this way. The people start taking an interest. I try to reclaim my rights. I bombard it with messages, each more vindictive than the last. It doesn't react and settles behind the ballot box.
"Please!" Go ahead, it announces to us calmly.
I'm left speechless. The survivors step up to the ballots.
"Who are you going to vote for," a young woman asks another.
Their eyes turn toward me.
"Definitely not for that stupid cow," he murmurs to her.
She seems to nod. I get the feeling they're both stabbing me. I feel humiliated. I no longer have control. I keep quiet. I'm on a knife's edge. One more word could give them yet more reasons to hate me. I have the horrible feeling this AI is mocking me. It's a masquerade. Jochem Maas comes out of nowhere. Nobody knows him. Nobody's seen him. There's a strong chance he's on the upper floor, hidden in a closet, praying someone comes to save him. There's no reason for him to be elected. None. I have to trust them.
Suddenly, Siska hails me.
"Madam Payet, you're the only one left!"
Everyone observes me. They're pressed against one another. I do believe I've been cast aside. I try to keep hope and I step up to the ballots. There's my name and my opponent's. I select mine. The virtual object sticks to my hand when I set it on the stack. I take an envelope. I try to slip my vote into it, but the projections repel each other like two magnets. At my back, a stifled snicker tenses me up. Am I that stupid? Siska points me toward the booth. Only sheltered from prying eyes can I fulfill my duty as a citizen. I take refuge there. I try not to crack. I feel like a child parodying democracy in front of her stuffed animals, but I have no other choice. I have to comply. I don't know what Siska is capable of. Coming out, I stay dignified before those who don't take their eyes off me, and when the envelope I drag along with me finally drops into the electoral box, Siska straightens up solemnly.
"Vote cast! it pronounces."
Then it closes the transparent box, whose walls go cloudy.
"Time for the count."
Two empty gauges surge from the void, beside the ballot box. Each bears the name of one of the candidates: *Cécile Payet* and *Jochem Maas*. Siska sets its hand on the lid and the gauges fill drop by drop. One vote for me. One vote for my opponent. Another one for him. I hold my breath. I get the feeling Siska is playing with my nerves, but things speed up. The election is over. I received only a single vote: my own. I'm flabbergasted. I stagger as if stunned by an uppercut. I want to leap at their throats, these idiots. How can you be so utterly lacking in sense? It's not with them that we'll save the city. I hold my head. They stay silent while Siska announces Mister Maas's victory with great enthusiasm. It disgusts me. It makes no sense at all. Around us, the AR projections finally vanish. The cab returns to its drab reality and Siska has faded away. Where the booths stood, against the freight lift doors, lies the lifeless body of Mister Mertens. I'd completely forgotten about him. My eyes go wide. My interface tells me he succumbed to his injuries. He gave his life to protect me. I swallow, but my heart doesn't have time to tighten in my chest before a question forces itself on me: we were ten to vote and yet, we're only nine now. So, who's the tenth?
It's surely him...

Yet, to my great surprise, the freight lift doors finally open onto a long, wide corridor where darkness reigns. We rejoice, but no one ventures into it.
Suddenly, as if to invite us — to draw us in — the bunker comes to life. One by one, the neon lights come on. They reveal a universe of concrete where a series of glass doors serve offices and meeting rooms. The ventilation purrs in the silence. A vacuum drone suddenly bursts from a corridor crossing ours. I jump, even though I know it's harmless. It glides across the floor, zigzagging, bumps into a wall, then disappears at a junction. I'd find it funny if the situation called for it, but its presence surprises me more than anything. 
"Right, are you following me?" the angry one throws out, addressing the others.
They all look lost. Part of me can't help rejoicing at it. The moment may be ill-chosen, but I'm going to let them fend for themselves. They'll eventually come to their senses when they realize the deception. I'm sure of it. So I watch them rush into the bunker without daring to meet my gaze. I can clearly see they're ashamed. Their soles squeak on the waxed floor. I follow them. They advance gropingly, open doors at random, and are surprised to be left to their own devices. They don't speak to me. They seem to have forgotten me. It quietly makes me laugh, once again. They scatter, regroup, and get angry. They don't know where to go, but I do! This floor is the command center. They won't find much of interest here. If they want answers, they'll simply have to follow me. At the end of the corridor, I spot the armored door giving access to the control room. That's where I want to go, because I've got only one idea left in my head: contacting the neighboring cities.
